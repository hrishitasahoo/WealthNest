Good to Know

Rule of 72
Roughly how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the annual return rate. At 8% return, money doubles in about 9 years.
Inflation erodes value
Cash sitting idle loses purchasing power every year. At 6% inflation, ₹100 today is worth about ₹56 in 10 years.
Emergency fund first
Most planners suggest covering 3–6 months of expenses before investing aggressively, so a setback doesn't force you to sell investments early.
Start date matters more than amount
Because of compounding, investing a smaller amount for longer often beats investing a larger amount for a shorter period.

About SIP

A Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) means investing a fixed amount regularly — usually monthly — into a mutual fund, instead of putting in a lump sum at once. Over time, this can average out the ups and downs of the market rather than betting everything on one entry point.

Good to know: Returns from mutual funds are market-linked, not guaranteed. Starting early — even with a small amount — usually matters more than starting big later, because of how compounding works over a longer period.

About Fixed Deposits

A Fixed Deposit (FD) is a lump sum you deposit with a bank for a set period at a fixed interest rate, agreed upfront. It's one of the lowest-risk ways to grow money in India, which is also why the returns are generally modest compared to market-linked options.

Good to know: Breaking an FD before it matures usually costs you a penalty on the interest earned. Interest from an FD is also taxable as per your income slab — it isn't tax-free.

About Compound Interest

Compound interest is calculated on both your original amount and the interest that's already been added to it — so your money grows faster the longer it stays invested, compared to simple interest which only ever grows on the original amount.

Good to know: Compounding frequency matters — monthly compounding grows your money slightly faster than yearly compounding, even at the same annual rate, because interest gets added (and starts earning its own interest) more often.

About Setting a Savings Goal

A vague goal like "save more" is hard to act on. A specific target amount and date turns it into a number you can actually plan around — a fixed monthly contribution you either hit or you don't.

Good to know: If the required monthly amount feels too high, it's usually more realistic to extend the timeline than to abandon the goal. A smaller, consistent contribution beats an ambitious one you can't sustain.
